## What You're Actually Signing Up For

Four sections: **FAR, AUD, REG, and TCP** (TCP replaced BEC in the 2024 Evolution of the CPA Exam). Each one is a separate exam window, and once you pass your first section, you've got 30 months to clear the other three.

AICPA estimates **300 to 400 total study hours** across all four sections — roughly 75 to 100 hours each. If you're working full time, that's 10 to 15 weeks per section at 8 to 10 hours per week. Not impossible, but there's no room to coast.

Pass rates sit around 45 to 55% per section. Most people who fail aren't failing because they're not smart enough. They're failing because their study plan fell apart.

---

## The Order That Makes Sense in 2026

No sequence is officially required, but order matters. Here's what works for most candidates:

1. **FAR first** — Hardest and broadest. Tackle it while your motivation is high and your time buffer is longest.
2. **AUD second** — Builds on FAR concepts. Most candidates need 60 to 70 hours here.
3. **REG third** — Tax-heavy and dense, but focused. Easier to study in concentrated blocks.
4. **TCP fourth** — The newest section. Covers tax compliance and planning in depth. If you have a tax background, it'll feel familiar.

Strong tax background? Flip TCP and REG. Coming from an audit role? AUD first makes sense. Adjust around your strengths — just don't keep pushing FAR to the back of the line.

## How to Build Your Study Schedule

### Work Backwards From Your Exam Date

Pick your test date first. Then count backwards.

Targeting FAR in 12 weeks means you need roughly 96 hours of study time. At 8 hours per week, that's exactly 12 weeks. At 10 hours per week, you finish with a two-week buffer for review. That buffer isn't optional — use it.

**A realistic 12-week FAR plan:**

- **Weeks 1–4:** Financial accounting and reporting (governmental, nonprofit, leases, revenue recognition)
- **Weeks 5–7:** Consolidations, foreign currency, derivatives
- **Weeks 8–9:** Governmental accounting deep dive — this is where most people lose points
- **Week 10:** Full content review pass
- **Weeks 11–12:** Mock exams, weak-area drilling, final review

Don't skip the buffer. FAR has 66 MCQs and 8 task-based simulations in a 4-hour window. You need timed practice, not just content coverage.

### Protect Your Study Blocks

Two focused hours beats five distracted ones. If you're working full time, your windows are probably mornings before work, lunch breaks, and evenings. Pick two of those three and protect them.

**What actually works for working candidates:**

- 1 hour before work (content review)
- 1 hour after work (practice questions)
- 3 to 4 hours on Saturday or Sunday (mock exam or deep review)

That's 10 to 11 hours per week. Enough to pass if you're consistent.

## Section-by-Section Time Estimates

Here's a realistic breakdown of hours and the hardest topics per section:

- **FAR — 100 to 120 hours.** Hardest topics: governmental accounting, consolidations.
- **AUD — 60 to 75 hours.** Hardest topics: risk assessment, internal controls.
- **REG — 80 to 95 hours.** Hardest topics: individual tax, business entities.
- **TCP — 70 to 85 hours.** Hardest topics: tax planning strategies, compliance.

These are averages. If governmental accounting is new to you, add 10 hours to FAR. If you've been doing tax work for three years, cut 15 from REG. Be honest about where you're starting from.

## The Study Method That Actually Moves the Needle

### Don't Just Read. Test Yourself.

Reading your review materials feels productive. It mostly isn't. Active recall — forcing yourself to retrieve information — is what actually builds retention.

The sequence that works:

1. **Read a topic section** (30 to 45 minutes)
2. **Do 20 to 30 MCQs on that topic immediately** — not at the end of the week
3. **Review every wrong answer** — understand *why*, not just *what*
4. **Return to weak topics** at the start of your next session before moving forward

This loop feels slower than passive reading at first. It's faster in the long run because you're not re-learning the same material three times.

### Track Your Weak Areas

Most candidates know they're weak somewhere. Few actually track it. If you're hitting 60% on consolidations and 85% on revenue recognition, your next study block should be consolidations — not more revenue recognition.

## Mock Exams: When and How Many

Most candidates do too few mock exams, too late.

**The right approach:**

- First mock exam at **end of week 6** — halfway through your study window. You'll probably fail it. That's the point. It shows you exactly where your gaps are while you still have time to fix them.
- Second mock exam at **week 10**.
- Final mock exam at **week 11**, under real conditions: timed, no interruptions, no notes.

The CPA exam is 4 hours per section. If you've never sat through a 4-hour timed session before exam day, the fatigue alone will cost you points.

## The Mistakes That Sink Candidates

**Starting with AUD or TCP instead of FAR.** FAR is the hardest section. Don't save it for when you're already tired.

**Studying without a deadline.** "I'll schedule the exam when I feel ready" means you'll never feel ready. Schedule first. The deadline creates urgency.

**Ignoring task-based simulations.** MCQs are 50% of your score. TBSs are the other 50%. Most candidates over-prepare on MCQs and show up underprepared for simulations. Practice both.

**Re-reading instead of practicing.** If you've read the same chapter twice and you're still missing questions, reading it a third time won't fix it. Do more practice questions.

**Burning out in month two.** The candidates who pass are usually the ones who studied at a sustainable pace — not the ones who went 14 hours a day for three weeks and then stopped. Consistent 8 to 10 hours per week beats inconsistent sprints every time.

## FAQs

**How many hours do I need to study for the CPA exam?**
Most candidates need 300 to 400 total hours across all four sections. FAR typically takes the most time (100 to 120 hours), while AUD tends to take the least (60 to 75 hours). Your actual hours will vary based on your accounting background and how recently you covered the material.

**What order should I take the CPA exam sections in 2026?**
Most candidates do best starting with FAR — it's the broadest and most difficult section. AUD and REG typically follow, with TCP last. If you have a strong tax background, moving TCP earlier makes sense. There's no single right order, but don't keep delaying FAR.

**How long does it take to pass all four CPA sections?**
Studying consistently at 8 to 10 hours per week, most candidates can pass one section every 10 to 14 weeks. Finishing all four in 12 to 18 months is realistic if you're working full time. You have 30 months from your first passing score to complete all four.

**What's the best way to study for the CPA exam while working full time?**
Protect two focused study blocks per day rather than trying to grind through long stretches. Mornings work well for content review; evenings work well for practice questions. Keep one longer weekend block — 3 to 4 hours — for mock exams or deep review. Consistency matters more than total daily hours.

**How many mock exams should I take before each CPA section?**
At minimum, three per section: one at the halfway point of your study window, one two weeks before your exam date, and one final simulation a week out under real timed conditions. Most candidates take too few, too late. Starting mock exams early shows you where your gaps are while you still have time to close them.

**What topics should I prioritize in FAR?**
Governmental accounting is the most commonly missed area in FAR and carries significant weight — prioritize it even if it feels unfamiliar. Consolidations, revenue recognition, and lease accounting are also high-yield. If you're consistently above 75% on practice questions in a topic, move on and put that time toward your weaker areas.

**Is a review course enough, or do I need additional study tools?**
A structured review course (Becker, Wiley, Surgent, or similar) is the foundation — don't skip it. Supplemental tools are useful for active recall and targeted weak-area drilling.
